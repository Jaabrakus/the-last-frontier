# Two Fires: core history research (track report)

**Confidence key:** High means primary sources or several good secondary sources agree. Medium means one decent secondary source, or sources that differ on details. Low means local tradition, genealogy or a single weak page.

**Limits of this pass:** the web-search budget ran out partway through, so some leads are marked unverified. I edited no files and ran no builds.

## 1. German Flatts, 12 November 1757, and the second raid of 30 April 1758

**Facts**
- **The force.** François-Marie Picoté de Belestre led about 300 men: troupes de la marine (French colonial regulars), Canadian militia and allied warriors. Vaudreuil, the governor of New France, sent him to the north bank of the Mohawk near the mouth of West Canada Creek. (High)
  - Sources: https://www.biographi.ca/en/bio/picote_de_belestre_francois_marie_4E.html and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attack_on_German_Flatts_(1757)
  - **Which nations the warriors came from is not identified** in anything I could reach. The likely source is the French summary itself (NYCD vol. 10, pp. 672–674).
- **The route.** They left Lachine on 20 October, went up the St Lawrence and along Lake Ontario to the Oswego River, then over the Oneida Carry to the Mohawk. They attacked at about 3 a.m. on 12 November. (Medium; Wikipedia, citing Benton's History of Herkimer County)
- **The settlement.** About 60 houses, about 300 settlers, 5 blockhouses and 75 militia. (Medium) A local history says only "about thirty houses" (Low): https://herkimer.nygenweb.net/herktown/herkpanorama.html
- **The losses.** About 40 killed (some say 40–50) and about 150 captured, including Johan Jost Petrie (called the "mayor") and a surgeon. About 60 houses, barns and a mill were burned. (High for the order of magnitude)
- **The French claims.** The French summary of 28 November 1757 says no attackers were killed and that M. de Lorimier and 3 or 4 warriors were wounded. It claims 3,000 cattle, 3,000 sheep, 1,500 horses and very large sums in coin and goods. My judgement is that the livestock and money figures are inflated; treat them as boasts. https://www.herkimer.nygenweb.net/gerflats/gerflatsattacks.html
- **The forts.** Fort Herkimer (also called Fort Kouari or Kaouri) stood on the **south** bank, built around the Herkimer stone house of about 1740. (Medium) https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Herkimer
  - The fort was not taken. About 50 men came out on 13 November and were driven back.
  - The garrison is given as about 250 by one source and 350 by the French: https://schenectadyhistory.org/resources/mvgw/history/044.html
- **The warning nobody believed.** At George Croghan's inquiry at Fort Herkimer on 30 November 1757, the Oneida sachem Conaghquieson said:
  - the Oneida had passed on a warning about 15 days before, which came through "Swegatchi" (Oswegatchie) Indians;
  - he had urged the Germans to gather at their fort, and they laughed at him;
  - a confirming wampum belt arrived the day before the attack and was disbelieved.
  - Hanyost Herkimer and Rudolph Shoemaker interpreted. (High: primary testimony, NYCD) Same two sources as above.
- **The captives.** Most were later exchanged. (Medium) One genealogy record says Catherine Frank and 6 of her 7 children were held in Montreal until the war ended, while her husband and eldest son, who were fighting, were not taken. (Low) https://sarpatriots.sar.org/patriot/display/161572
- **The 30 April 1758 raid.** About 80 warriors and 4 Frenchmen attacked. An Oneida warned Captain Nicholas Herkimer at noon, so most people reached the fort before the attack at about 3–4 p.m.
  - About 30–33 inhabitants were killed. Lieutenant Hair of the rangers was wounded.
  - Five waggoners held a house by knocking attackers off the stairs.
  - A woman who had been scalped survived and said Onondaga were among the attackers.
  - (High: NY Mercury, 22 May 1758, in Documentary History of NY vol. 1, pp. 522–523, via the two pages above)
- **A thread you can use.** Belestre surrendered Detroit to Robert Rogers on 29 November 1760. The man who burned the Flatts handed Detroit to the man who burned Saint-Francis. (High; the DCB page above)

**Mission ideas**
1. *The Belt Nobody Believed* (autumn 1757). Carry or hear the Oneida warning and try to get the Flatts into the fort. They laugh, as they did historically. The Oneida content is a [[consultation slot]]; with the slot empty, a German neighbour can retell the rumour.
2. *Three in the Morning*. A night attack on the north-bank blockhouses. The fort across the river holds and sends 50 men who fall back, so the player sees that help is near and still does not come.
3. *The Inquiry* (30 November 1757, at Fort Herkimer). A text-dialogue investigation of who was warned and when. The interpreters are named and real.
4. *Five Waggoners* (30 April 1758). Defend one house on the stairs until the rangers come.

## 2. Captivity and adoption narratives

**James Smith** (captured May 1755, Pennsylvania; Kephart's 1915 edition of his 1799 narrative: https://gutenberg.org/cache/epub/44934/pg44934-images.html). High.
- He was taken on the road near Bedford: his horse threw him and his companion Arnold Vigoras was killed.
- He ran the gauntlet at Fort Duquesne and was beaten unconscious; a French doctor treated him. From the fort he saw the spoils of Braddock's defeat come in.
- At his adoption at a Caughnawaga (Kahnawake Mohawk) town on the Allegheny:
  - his hair was plucked except for a scalp lock, and his nose and ears were pierced;
  - three young women ducked him in the river, then he was dressed, seated on a bearskin and given a pipe and tomahawk;
  - he was told he had replaced a great man. https://historynet.com/james-smith-indian-captive-indian-fighter-revolutionary-patriot/
- He got lost following buffalo tracks and was teased for it. **His gun was taken away for about two years and he hunted with a bow.**
- His seasonal life included sugar making with elm-bark vessels, beaver taken at breathing holes under the ice, raccoon hunting in snow, and a night in a hollow log. Winter huts were built of logs, poles and bark.
- **The starving winter (February 1758)** with the elder Tecaughretanego and his 10-year-old son: Smith went two days without food, then killed a buffalo cow, and the elder gave a speech of thanks.
- In 1759 he got away near Montreal, was jailed by the French for about four months, was exchanged, and was home in 1760. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Smith_(frontiersman)
- **Why he matters for Two Fires:** his adoptive family were Kahnawake people, and he ended up near Montreal. He is the best documented link between a Canada-based Part 1 and the Ohio-country narratives.

**Mary Jemison** (captured 5 April 1758, Marsh Creek, Pennsylvania; Seaver's 1824 narrative: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/6960/pg6960-images.html). High.
- She was taken by Shawnee and French. When her shoes were swapped for moccasins, it signalled she would be kept alive. Her parents and siblings were killed.
- At Fort Duquesne the captors cleaned and painted scalps, and she recognised her family's hair.
- She was given to two Seneca sisters to replace **a brother** killed in the war. They sang a mourning lament that turned into a welcome, and she was **named at the adoption** (Dickewamis).
- She worked in the cornfields and later judged the women's work no harder than white women's. She practised her English prayers in secret.
- She married Sheninjee (Lenape) and walked 500 to 700 miles with her baby to the Genesee, swimming horses over flooded creeks.
- **After the 1763 peace, John Van Sice tried to seize her for the redemption bounty.** She hid for three days. When "the old king" wanted her taken to Niagara, her Seneca brother said he would kill her himself before letting her be taken by force. Her sister left a cake as a signal, and she hid at a spring until her brother came for her.

**Hugh Gibson** (captured July 1756 at Robinson's Fort, Sherman's Valley, Pennsylvania). Medium. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_Gibson_(American_pioneer)
- His mother was killed. He was adopted at Kittanning as a brother by Pisquetomen, washed in the river and renamed.
- He saw a warrior wearing his mother's scalp. During Armstrong's raid on Kittanning on 8 September 1756 he was kept back with the women.
- He was beaten for refusing a marriage, then forgiven.
- **He escaped on 16 March 1759** with Marie Le Roy, Barbara Leininger and David Brackenridge. They covered about 250 miles, nearly drowned, and reached Fort Pitt on 31 March.

**Charles Stuart** (captured at Great Cove, 1 November 1755). Medium. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Cove_massacre
- He ran the gauntlet at Kittanning, was passed to a Wyandot chief, and his children were given to families at Sandusky. He and his wife were sold to French priests at Detroit and returned in 1757.
- **He recorded Shingas (Lenape) explaining the war:** Braddock had said the English would inherit the land and that Braddock did not need Lenape help. This is the "why it is happening" the draft asks for. The published edition is Beverley Bond, Mississippi Valley Historical Review 13:1 (1926), pp. 58–81.

**Mary Campbell** (taken 21 May 1758, aged 10, by Lenape). Low to Medium: mostly family tradition. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Campbell_(colonial_settler)
- She was probably in Netawatwees' household at Cuyahoga Falls (the "Mary Campbell Cave"). One account has her hoeing with a deer-scapula hoe.
- She was returned through Bouquet and reached Fort Pitt on 28 November 1764.

**Thomas Gist** (captured at Grant's defeat, 14 September 1758). Medium. https://static-prod.lib.princeton.edu/republic/captivity-diary-1758/
- He was adopted by a Wyandot family at the Huron town opposite Detroit, was "well treated", and escaped after a year.

**Eunice Williams** (Deerfield, 1704). High.
- She was adopted at Kahnawake, took successive names, married a Mohawk man and forgot English. She refused every effort to bring her back, including her father's visit. https://newenglandhistoricalsociety.com/eunice-williams-unredeemed-captive/ (John Demos, *The Unredeemed Captive*)

**Gilbert family.** Not verified this pass. From memory: a Quaker family taken in 1780 and held at Niagara (Low). That is outside the 1757–75 window, so it belongs to "The Burning Valley" storyline.

**Mourning war and requickening.** The standard secondary source is Daniel Richter, "War and Culture: The Iroquois Experience," *William and Mary Quarterly* 40 (1983), pp. 528–559: https://oieahc.wm.edu/publications/wmq/browse/volume-40-1983/october-1983/ (High that this is the standard work). It covers the Haudenosaunee only. Lenape, Shawnee, Wyandot and Abenaki practice differed, so these details must be confirmed per nation.

**The 1764 Bouquet return.**
- Bouquet led about 1,500 men to the Tuscarawas and Muskingum in October 1764 and demanded all captives. More than 200 were handed over, and he was back at Fort Pitt by 28 November. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Bouquet
- Native families handed over captives in tears, followed the army and hunted to feed them on the way. https://explorepahistory.com/odocument.php%3FdocId=1-4-23.html (The Annual Register, from William Smith's 1765 account; High)
- A young Mingo man followed the Virginia woman he was married to, at the risk of his life (same source).
- Some captives had to be tied, some refused to eat, and some slipped back to the towns, Rhoda Boyd among them. https://www.nrafamily.org/content/throwback-thursday-colonel-henry-bouquet-frees-the-indian-captives (Medium)

**Mission ideas**
1. *The Gauntlet*: an arrival ordeal, survived rather than won (Smith, Stuart).
2. *Bow Only*: you get lost on your first hunt and lose your gun; the bow lessons follow. This is straight from Smith and fits the climbing, bow and tracking systems.
3. *The Starving Winter*: an old man with bad joints, a boy, crusted snow, two days hungry, and then the buffalo (Smith, 1758).
4. *The Redemption Men* (1763–64): bounty hunters come for you and you hide until a signal comes (Jemison). Or the reverse: you are tied and handed over at the Muskingum (Bouquet).
5. *Four Who Ran*: a 250-mile escape with three other captives, river crossings and hunger (Gibson, Le Roy, Leininger).

## 3. Rogers' raid on Odanak (Saint-Francis), 4 October 1759

**Facts**
- **The order.** Amherst issued it on 13 September. He told Rogers to take revenge but gave orders that "no women or children are killed or hurt." Accounts that the Abenaki had mistreated Captain Kennedy and Lieutenant Hamilton, who were taken while under a flag of truce, were part of the anger behind it. (High) https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Francis_raid
- **Getting there.** About 200–220 men left Crown Point in 17 whaleboats; about 41 turned back. The boats hidden at Missisquoi Bay were found by the French on about 25 September. There followed a nine-day march through spruce bog. 142 men reached the town; about 25 were Stockbridge allies. (High) https://historynet.com/rogers-rangers-risky-1759-st-francis-raid/
- **The attack.** It began before dawn. The town was mostly women, children and old people. The church was looted and burned and the storehouses kept for food. Rogers claimed to find about 600 scalps, "mostly English."
- **The dead, which are disputed:**
  - Rogers claimed about 200 killed and 20 captured, of whom he kept 5 or 6.
  - French accounts give about 30, about 20 of them women and children.
  - Historians' estimates run from about 65 to 140.
  - **Do not use Rogers' 200 as fact.** (High that it is disputed)
- **Rangers' losses.** In the attack, 1 Stockbridge man was killed and Captain Ogden and 6 others were wounded. Five captives were freed. (Medium)
- **Abenaki oral tradition**, recorded by Gordon Day from Elvine Obomsawin (1959) and Theophile Panadis (1961):
  - a Mahican stranger, Samadagwis, came to the council house during a dance the night before and warned them;
  - many people hid at a brook, while the men held the council house like a fort;
  - Abenaki pursuers pushed the rangers so hard they had no time to hunt;
  - Samadagwis was found wounded and killed after a mock baptism.
  - Transcript: https://static1.squarespace.com/static/62ab3d3a7aae4774cdfcd747/t/641b790a815da6200cc22bba/1679522058919/rogers-raid-oral-tradition.pdf. The scholarly analysis is Marge Bruchac's "Reading Abenaki Traditions and European Records of Rogers' Raid," https://repository.upenn.edu/anthro_papers/155. **This is Abenaki community knowledge; it belongs to the consultation slot.**
- **The retreat.** It went over Lake Memphremagog toward the Connecticut River, with the men split into small parties.
  - About 40–49 rangers were killed, captured or starved. They ate belts, moccasins and powder horns, and there are reports of eating human flesh.
  - The relief party at the Connecticut River left with the food shortly before Rogers arrived; the timing is reported differently.
  - Rogers rafted down to Fort No. 4 on about 31 October and sent food back. (High for the outline, Medium for details)
- **The town today.** Odanak is a living First Nation with the Musée des Abénakis: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odanak

**Mission ideas** (all from inside the town, all [[consultation slot]])
1. *The Stranger at the Dance*: the night-before warning. Whether the player believes it, and spreads it, decides who survives. This mirrors the ignored warning at German Flatts.
2. *The Council House*: hold the big house until the light comes while families run for the brook.
3. *Push Them*: harry the retreating rangers so they cannot hunt. This is a pursuit mission that reverses the rangers' starvation.
4. For a ranger-side player in a separate storyline: *The Boats Are Gone*, then the bog, then the starving retreat.

## 4. 1763–68: Pontiac's War, the Proclamation, Paxton, the Black Boys, the Stamp Act, Johnson, Fort Stanwix

**Pontiac's War.** High. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontiac%27s_War
- Amherst cut presents and gunpowder in 1761. Fort Detroit was attacked in May 1763.
- Forts fell from 16 May to 19 June, including Michilimackinac through the lacrosse-game ruse.
- Bouquet won at Bushy Run on 5 August. **Seneca ambushed a portage wagon train at Devil's Hole near Niagara on 14 September 1763**, killing more than 70; this one is in New York.
- At Johnson's Niagara congress in 1764 the Seneca ceded the portage. Croghan treated with Pontiac in 1765, and Pontiac made peace at Fort Ontario on 25 July 1766.

**Royal Proclamation, 7 October 1763.** High. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Proclamation_of_1763
- It set a line on the Appalachian watershed. It banned settlement west of the line, banned private purchases of Indian land (purchases only at public meetings with Crown officials) and licensed the trade. It was meant to be temporary.
- **For "The Ford", the ban on private purchases is the relevant part in New York.**

**Paxton Boys.** High. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paxton_Boys
- On 14 December 1763 they killed 6 people at Conestoga Town. On 27 December they killed the 14 survivors in the Lancaster workhouse, 20 dead in all.
- In February 1764 several hundred marched on Philadelphia to kill the Moravian Lenape and Mohican refugees there. Franklin raised a militia and negotiated with them on 7 February. No one was prosecuted.

**Black Boys rebellion, 1765.** High. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Black_Boys_rebellion
- 5 March 1765: an 81-horse pack train of Baynton, Wharton & Morgan goods for Croghan reached Pawling's Tavern. Locals saw what looked like scalping knives.
- 6 March: Smith and about 10 men with blackened faces destroyed the goods at Sideling Hill in a snowstorm.
- Sergeant McGlashan's 42nd Regiment patrol arrested suspects.
- 11 March: 200–300 armed men surrounded Fort Loudoun and forced Lieutenant Grant to free the prisoners. **Grant kept nine confiscated guns.**
- 5–6 May: a firefight at Widow Barr's. James Brown was shot in the thigh, the only casualty of the whole rising.
- May–June: Smith and Justice William Smith issued their own passes for goods on the road.
- 28 May: the Black Boys captured Grant, who promised to return the guns.
- November: two nights of firing at the fort, and the guns were handed over through a third party.
- **1769:** Smith's own account says the Black Boys freed prisoners at Fort Bedford. No other record confirms it (Low).
- **Fort Loudoun was a wooden stockade about 100 feet square, not stone.** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Loudoun_(Pennsylvania)
- Patrick Spero's *Frontier Rebels* (2018) says the goods were for a diplomatic expedition to open trade with Pontiac. https://claremontreviewofbooks.com/digital/the-black-boys-rebellion/ and https://ebsco.com/research-starters/history/black-boys-rebellion

**Stamp Act.** High. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stamp_Act_1765
- Passed 22 March 1765, in force 1 November 1765, repealed 18 March 1766.
- **Deeds, legal papers and licences needed stamps**, and cards and dice were taxed. The New York stamp distributor James McEvers resigned. The Stamp Act Congress met in New York in October 1765.
- Not verified: Albany's Sons of Liberty in 1766 and Henry Van Schaack as a target. This is a strong lead for Albany missions and worth checking.

**Sir William Johnson.** https://www.biographi.ca/en/bio/johnson_william_4E.html
- He ran Indian affairs from Johnson Hall, Johnstown. Mary (Molly) Brant was his partner, with eight children who survived.
- He took a 130,000-acre grant from the Canajoharie Mohawk and about 40,000 acres from the Oneida (1765). **His own Charlotte Creek purchase was abandoned because of the 1768 line.**
- He died on 11 July 1774 during a council at Johnson Hall. (High)
- His son inherited enslaved people (about 20). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_John_Johnson,_2nd_Baronet (Medium)

**Treaty of Fort Stanwix, 5 November 1768.** High for these facts. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Fort_Stanwix and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_of_Property
- Johnson and Croghan negotiated with commissioners from Pennsylvania, Virginia and New Jersey present. Payment was £10,460 7s 3d.
- The line ran to the Tennessee River, which gave up Kentucky and most of West Virginia. **The Six Nations sold hunting lands of the Shawnee and Lenape, who did not sign.** This set up Dunmore's War in 1774.
- **In New York the line ran from near Fort Stanwix along Wood Creek and Canada Creek to the Unadilla and Delaware rivers.** Land east of it became purchasable, which is exactly The Ford's land rush.
- The "suffering traders" got the Indiana grant for their Pontiac's War losses; Samuel Wharton and William Trent gained most. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indiana_Company
- **Not verified:** the attendance of about 3,000 Native people that is often quoted.
- Background: the Mohawk had protested land frauds since Hendrick's "Covenant Chain is broken" speech of 1753. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hendrick_Theyanoguin
- Not verified: George Klock's deeds from the Canajoharie Mohawk. This would be a strong real model for Silas Vane.

**Mission ideas**
1. *Scalping Knives at Pawling's*: inspect the train, then *Sideling Hill* in snow.
2. *Nine Guns*: the long fight with Lieutenant Grant (a siege, a kidnapping, getting the guns back), on the existing stockade.
3. *The Pass*: run goods with a forged or real Black Boys pass.
4. *Devil's Hole*: a portage-road ambush (Seneca, so a slot) or the teamsters' side.
5. *Unstamped*: deeds signed between November 1765 and March 1766 are legally doubtful. Use that as Vane's lever over the doubly sold lease.

## 5. 1770–75: Tryon County

High unless marked.
- **Tryon County** was created on 24 March 1772 from Albany County. Its seat was Johnstown, with a courthouse and jail built in 1772. Its districts were Mohawk, Palatine, Canajoharie, German Flatts and Kingsland. Sir John Johnson, Guy Johnson, Daniel Claus and John Butler were among its judges. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tryon_County,_New_York
- **The Committee of Safety** formed on 27 August 1774 with Christopher Yates as chairman. It required passes to enter or leave the county and arrested suspects. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tryon_County_Committee_of_Safety
- **Guy Johnson** succeeded Sir William in 1774 and left in May 1775 with about 120 Loyalists and about 90 Mohawk under John Deseronto. He held a council at Oswego in July and reached Montreal by mid-July; his wife died on the way. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guy_Johnson (Medium)
- **John Butler** left in 1775 with his sons Walter and Thomas. **His family was held by the Americans until an exchange in 1780.** https://www.biographi.ca/en/bio/butler_john_1796_4E.html
- **Sir John Johnson** was disarmed by Schuyler in January 1776 (about 3,000 troops, about 300 Loyalists disarmed). He fled to Montreal in May 1776 with about 170 followers, and his wife was held hostage.
- **Joseph Brant** went to German Flatts in April 1775, reached Montreal on 17 July and sailed for London on 11 November 1775. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Brant (Mohawk, so a slot)
- **The Oneida and Tuscarora** leaned to the Patriots, influenced by Samuel Kirkland, who worked among the Oneida from about 1764–66. Most Mohawk, Seneca, Cayuga and Onondaga sided with Britain. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oneida_people and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Kirkland
- **Not verified:** the Oneida neutrality declaration of 1775, the German Flatts and Albany councils of August 1775, the Onondaga council fire covered in 1777, and the liberty-pole fight at Caughnawaga (Fonda) in spring 1775.

**Mission ideas**
1. *Passes, Again*: the committee's checkpoints echo the Black Boys' passes of 1765. Smuggle someone out of the county.
2. *Guy Park, May 1775*: escort or stop the departure.
3. *Hostages*: the Butler family, who were left behind.
4. *The Last Council*: Johnson Hall, 11 July 1774, as an epilogue scene.

## Flags for the draft

1. **The geography of the raid.** The 1757 raid destroyed the **north-bank** settlement; Fort Herkimer on the **south** bank held.
   - The prologue puts the stockade-as-fort at the centre, with the homestead south-east of it. That makes it a near miss.
   - The homestead should sit on the north bank, or the player should cross to help.
   - "The Flatts are ash" is fine, but say the fort across the river stood.
2. **"Walked north."** The force came and left by the Oneida Carry, Oswego and Lake Ontario (Medium). That means west first, then by water down the St Lawrence. Most captives were later exchanged.
3. **Names.** "At first no name" conflicts with the narratives: Jemison and Gibson were named at the adoption itself. An earned adult name can still be a separate later step, but confirm that per nation.
4. **"A family who lost a son."** Jemison, a girl, replaced a brother. The person replaced need not match the adoptee's sex, which suits a custom character.
5. **The town and its burning are tied together.** Captives from German Flatts would most plausibly go to the mission towns of the Seven Nations of Canada: Kahnawake, Kanesatake, Akwesasne, Oswegatchie or Odanak (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_Nations_of_Canada).
   - **Only Odanak was burned in 1759.** The idea of "moving to another nation if one office says no" breaks Part 2's history unless the burning becomes fiction.
   - A Seneca town does not fit a German Flatts capture in 1757.
6. **Rogers' numbers.** Do not use 200 dead; give the 30-to-200 dispute. Abenaki memory holds a warning and many survivors.
7. **The "return" in 1760 versus 1764.** The well-documented forced, tearful return is **Bouquet in Ohio, 1764**. I found no source on how captives were handled at Kahnawake in 1760; D. Peter MacLeod's work on the treaty would be the place to check.
8. **The Black Boys were not a tax protest.**
   - They stopped arms and presents going to Native nations for Pontiac-era diplomacy. That is anti-Native and anti-imperial at once, and the text should show it.
   - They besieged one stockade to get back nine guns, and one man was wounded in the whole of 1765.
   - The Mountain Road's "stone and earth fort with cannon" is wrong for Fort Loudoun. **The existing stockade fits.**
9. **The 1768 treaty.** It sold mostly Shawnee and Lenape hunting land in the Ohio country. In the valley, it opened land east of the Wood Creek–Unadilla line. Rewrite "sells the land your town hunted on" to say which.
10. **Courts before 1772.** Until 1772 the valley was Albany County, so in 1765 the courts were Albany's. Johnstown becomes the county seat only in 1772.
11. **Missing from the draft.** There are enslaved people at Johnson Hall and in Albany households (Medium). Real models for Hollis and Company exist: Baynton, Wharton & Morgan, the Croghan schemes and the "suffering traders" grant.
12. **A structure you could use.** Both fires were preceded by a Native warning: ignored at the Flatts in 1757, heeded at Odanak in 1759. That mirror could be the story's spine. All three warners (Oneida, Mahican/Stockbridge, Abenaki memory) are consultation slots.
