# Experimental pixel protection options

Research review, 2026-10-07. The current [TBW2 implementation](pixel_policy_verification.md) carries recoverable restrictions and makes cooperating software refuse restricted uses. It has **not** been shown to impair a noncooperating model. That would require a separately optimized and evaluated adversarial perturbation. Adding arbitrary noise is not evidence of protection.

For this game's actual concern, neither reviewed method demonstrates that viewers or AI cannot infer combat rules, imitate game mechanics, or reconstruct/rebuild 3D assets from a public trailer. Caption disruption and edit degradation do not establish those outcomes. Hiding the HUD reduces exposed UI details; the visible combat, characters and world remain observable. Those separate objectives would need their own evaluations rather than a general “AI protection” label.

## What the two papers actually target

| Method | Intended effect | Relevant boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **VideoGuard**, arXiv:2508.03480 | Disrupt diffusion-based video edits and their temporal consistency | Targets an editing pipeline, not captioning or general AI understanding |
| **Protecting Your Video Content**, CVPR 2025 | Ramblings induce inaccurate captions; Mutes shorten or suppress captions | Targets video-language annotation, not every use of video or every model |

VideoGuard jointly optimizes video latents and then searches for pixel perturbations. Its reported experiments use FateZero, Tune-A-Video and Video-P2P. The paper reports approximately **30 minutes plus 40 minutes on one A100 80 GB** for its two optimization stages, evaluates 80 text/video pairs, and acknowledges limitations on conditional editors and long videos. I found no author implementation link in the paper/arXiv entry; code availability, dependencies and a reusable code license remain unverified. The arXiv publication license is not a software license. This is a research reproduction project rather than a ready CPU plugin. [Primary paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.03480v1).

The CVPR paper mainly assumes access to model weights. Its limited cross-model result uses Video-LLaMA to Video-Vicuna. In that experiment, both Mute variants still have **0% EOS/empty-output rate**, although captions become shorter. Its Mute transfer procedure evaluates both models during 200 optimization iterations and selects the shortest output; this is not an untouched holdout test. The appendix's format conversion uses lossless FFV1, while its “compression” experiment resizes 224×224 to 112×112 with that transformation included in optimization. These results do not establish survival of ordinary H.264/social-platform recompression or protection against arbitrary current models. [Primary paper and appendices D.6/D.8](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.21824v1).

## Implementation, license and hardware feasibility

The [CVPR author repository](https://github.com/ttthhl/Protecting_Your_Video_Content) points to Video-LLaMA and its 7B pretrained checkpoint. Its actual [attack code](https://github.com/ttthhl/Protecting_Your_Video_Content/blob/main/attack.py) hard-codes CUDA, backpropagates through the model, and samples **eight 224×224 frames**. The supplied [script](https://github.com/ttthhl/Protecting_Your_Video_Content/blob/main/attack.sh) runs up to 500 iterations with a 16/255 perturbation bound. That bound is a research setting, not a guarantee of invisibility. The writer exports sampled frames at a default **5 fps**, using FFV1, without an audio path. It cannot directly replace our full-duration 60 fps export pipeline.

The inherited [environment](https://github.com/DAMO-NLP-SG/Video-LLaMA/blob/main/environment.yml) specifies Python 3.9, CUDA/PyTorch 1.12.1, Transformers 4.28 and bitsandbytes 0.37, plus video-processing dependencies. It needs an isolated environment and a compatibility review, not installation into the current watermark runtime. The repository contains inherited BSD license notices, but the upstream Video-LLaMA README explicitly describes a **non-commercial research preview** and recommends A100 40/80 GB or A6000 for inference. Base language-model/checkpoint terms also require review before a public or commercial service. The BSD notices alone do not clear the whole model stack. [Upstream usage, hardware and terms](https://github.com/DAMO-NLP-SG/Video-LLaMA).

The current local runtime reports `arm64`, 10 logical CPUs, `torch.cuda.is_available() == False`, and `torch.backends.mps.is_available() == False`. This is an observation about the accessible runtime, not a claim that the machine physically lacks a GPU. Neither supplied method is presently a small runnable CPU feature here. Porting CUDA calls is only the first issue; model storage, gradient memory and many repeated forward/backward passes remain. A reduced-frame/small-model CPU experiment could illustrate target-specific feature disruption, but would not reproduce these papers or prove video-caption protection.

**Recommended first research target:** captioning, if the desired outcome is to make automated descriptions less useful. It matches the requested “AI cannot read/use this” concern more closely than a diffusion-editor attack. Treat it as an isolated experiment with explicit model and hardware scope. A VideoGuard reproduction should be a separate GPU-budgeted project if resistance to generative edits is the priority.

## Bounded proof of concept

1. Freeze a **2–4 second owned/authorized gameplay clip**, its original audio/timestamps and a few factual reference descriptions. Choose an authorized open-weight captioning model with reviewed weights/code licenses. First measure one inference and one input-gradient step; set wall-time/memory limits before a longer optimization. Do not download a dataset or train a new foundation model.
2. Compare the clean clip, TBW2-only clip, optimized perturbation, and equal-strength random-noise control using identical prompts/seeds. Start at the model's small frame preprocessing size as a feasibility screen. Producing a continuous full-frame-rate video and preserving audio is a separate required export step. Evaluate alternate frame sampling so success on eight selected frames is not mistaken for whole-video protection.
3. Freeze the perturbation before evaluating an **independent held-out model**, additional prompts and different frame sampling. Measure caption factual usefulness and errors, not just output length. For an editing experiment, compare matched edit prompts/seeds and temporal consistency instead. Any claimed protection against mechanics inference or 3D reconstruction would require a separate benchmark of those tasks. Record unchanged or failed protection as results.
4. Repeat after actual H.264 CRF18/28 reencoding, resizing and a small crop. Test perturbation plus TBW2 in both application orders: either may damage the other. Require exact UUID/deny recovery as well as the separately measured adversarial effect.
5. Inspect native-speed playback for temporal flicker, HUD/text readability and visible artifacts; record PSNR/SSIM, perturbation size, audio/timestamp preservation, runtime and peak memory. Only consider integration if the stated target-model effect, held-out result, delivery transforms and viewing-quality checks all meet predeclared criteria. A failure of transfer stays a target-specific result, not a universal shield.

No new models were downloaded, no external media uploads were made, and neither research repository was executed in this review. The current engine/service and delivered videos were not altered. A useful result would be a measured reduction in a named model's performance on stated tests; neither proposal can justify “no AI can copy or use this.”
